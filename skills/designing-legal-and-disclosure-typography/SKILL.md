# Designing legal and disclosure typography

Disclosure text often becomes the smallest, lowest-contrast typography in an interface precisely when users most need to understand consequences. Use this skill for terms, pricing caveats, consent language, risk warnings, eligibility conditions, and regulated notices.

## Decision ownership

Own readability floor, prominence relative to the qualified claim/action, grouping, measure, emphasis, link treatment, and responsive behavior. Decide which disclosures must be proximal and persistent versus available through expansion.

## Inputs and evidence

Collect legal requirements, regulator or policy guidance, actual disclosure copy, user decision context, claim prominence, target demographics, zoom, localization, and dark/high-contrast themes. Identify information that changes the meaning of a price, consent, or risk statement.

## Procedure

Place material disclosures near the claim or action they qualify. Use readable text size, adequate contrast, line height, and measure; do not rely on tiny typography to reduce visual weight. Establish emphasis hierarchy within long disclosures so critical conditions are scannable without misleading selective bolding.

Ensure links remain identifiable and keyboard accessible. On mobile, avoid burying mandatory content behind ambiguous truncation or a collapsed region that users are unlikely to notice. If progressive disclosure is allowed, signal both existence and significance.

## Failure topology

Legal text can technically exist while being functionally unreadable. Excessive all-caps, tiny size, dense measure, or low contrast turns disclosure into camouflage. Another failure is separating a caveat from the price or action it modifies, causing users to interpret the primary statement incorrectly.

## Falsification

Test 200–400% zoom, mobile widths, high contrast, long localization, and screen-reader order. Ask users to identify material conditions before completing the related action. Compare visual prominence of the claim and caveat to ensure hierarchy is truthful rather than deceptive.

## Output contract

Produce a `legal-and-disclosure-typography-contract` containing readability minima, proximity rules, emphasis hierarchy, link behavior, progressive-disclosure constraints, responsive treatment, accessibility tests, and examples tied to real decision contexts.

## Handoffs

Use consent/privacy skills for substantive flow, `designing-content-fidelity-audits` for wording verification, `verifying-typography-under-zoom` for scaling, and trust/high-stakes specialists when disclosure affects consequential decisions.
